Let $q \in \mathbb{N}$ with $q \ge 2$ be an integer base. A real number $x \in \mathbb{R}$ is rational if and only if its canonical $q$-ary expansion is eventually periodic.

# Proposition 1
Let $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ be a rational number, expressed as $x = \frac{a}{b}$ with $a \in \mathbb{Z}$, $b \in \mathbb{N}$, and $\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

Decomposing $x = \lfloor x \rfloor + \{x\}$, where the integer part $\lfloor x \rfloor \in \mathbb{Z}$ possesses a finite $q$-ary representation, it suffices to analyze the fractional part $\{x\} = \frac{r_0}{b} \in [0, 1)$, with $r_0 \in \{0, 1, \dots, b - 1\}$. Without loss of generality, assume $0 \le x < 1$.

By the [[Proof that Euclid's Algorithm Works|Division Algorithm]], dividing $q \cdot r_n$ by $b$ at each step $n \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0\}$ uniquely defines the next digit $d_{n+1}$ and remainder $r_{n+1}$:
$$
\begin{gather}
q \cdot r_n = d_{n+1} b + r_{n+1}, \quad d_{n+1} = \left\lfloor \frac{q \cdot r_n}{b} \right\rfloor \in \{0, 1, \dots, q - 1\}, \quad r_{n+1} \in \{0, 1, \dots, b - 1\} \tag{1}
\end{gather}
$$
The expansion of $x$ generated by step 1 evaluates to: 
$$
\begin{gather}
x = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{d_n}{q^n} \tag{2}
\end{gather}
$$
Because each remainder $r_n$ belongs to the finite set $\{0, 1, \dots, b - 1\}$ of cardinality $b$, the [[Proof of the Pigeonhole Principle|Pigeonhole Principle]] guarantees that among the first $b + 1$ remainders $r_0, r_1, \dots, r_b$, at least two remainders must coincide. Let $j$ and $k$ be the minimal pair of indices such that $0 \le j < k \le b$ and $r_j = r_k$. Define the period length $p := k - j \ge 1$: 

Because the digit $d_{n+1}$ and the next remainder $r_{n+1}$ in step 1 are determined entirely by $r_n$, the equality $r_j = r_k$ implies by induction that: 
$$
\begin{gather}
r_{n+p} = r_n \quad \text{and} \quad d_{n+p} = d_n \quad \forall n > j \tag{3}
\end{gather}
$$
Step 3 demonstrates that after a non-repeating pre-period of length $j$, the sequence of digits repeats periodically with period $p$. Therefore, the $q$-ary expansion of $x$ is eventually periodic.

---
# Proposition 2
Conversely, suppose that the canonical $q$-ary expansion of a non-negative real number $x \ge 0$ is eventually periodic: after a pre-period of length $k \ge 0$, a block of $p \ge 1$ digits repeats indefinitely.

Then $x$ decomposes into a terminating rational prefix and an infinite repeating tail:
$$
\begin{gather}
x = \frac{A}{q^k} + \frac{1}{q^k} \sum_{i=1}^\infty \frac{D}{(q^p)^i} \tag{4}
\end{gather}
$$
where $A \in \mathbb{N} \cup \{0\}$ is the integer represented by the pre-period digits, and $D \in \{0, 1, \dots, q^p - 1\}$ is the integer represented by the repeating block of length $p$. Factoring $D$ out of the summation in step 4 isolates a geometric series:
$$
\begin{gather}
\sum_{i=1}^\infty \left(\frac{1}{q^p}\right)^i = \frac{1/q^p}{1 - 1/q^p} = \frac{1}{q^p - 1} \tag{5}
\end{gather}
$$
Substituting step 5 into step 4 gives: 
$$
\begin{gather}
x = \frac{A}{q^k} + \frac{D}{q^k (q^p - 1)} = \frac{A(q^p - 1) + D}{q^k (q^p - 1)} \tag{6}
\end{gather}
$$
Because $A, D, q, p, k$ are integers with $q \ge 2$, $p \ge 1$, and $k \ge 0$, both the numerator $A(q^p - 1) + D$ and the denominator $q^k (q^p - 1)$ are integers, with $q^k (q^p - 1) \in \mathbb{N}$. Consequently, $x \in \mathbb{Q}$ is a rational number.
$$
\begin{gather}
\textbf{Q.E.D.}
\end{gather}
$$
